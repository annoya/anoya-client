HomeScreen · _pickLocation · transportA different subscription — on purpose. All fourteen “Nexus Proxy” servers run over plain tcp, so this half of the line never appears on it even once; there would be nothing to show transport with. Here is a provider whose transports differ.

Protocol, transport and security — as a list, like in Happ. Three facts separated by dots: VLESS · TCP · Reality. The separator is ours, not a vertical bar: the dot already separates everything else in the app, and introducing a second separator for one line means starting a second language.

The address is no longer in the line. It used to come last and was justified by telling apart identically named entries. But identical names are the provider's oversight, not a problem the interface is obliged to solve for it; paying for it with an endpoint on every row is expensive. The space it took went to what actually tells servers of one provider apart.

TCP is now written out. Previously it was hidden as “a default that says nothing” — true while the address stood next to it and space was at a premium. In a list of three, omitting one item reads not as “default” but as “not determined”.

Security is always named, including its absence. Reality, TLS — or No TLS, because “not stated” and “none” are not the same thing for a VPN client, and silence here would read as the former.

One place where we do not repeat the engine verbatim: httpupgrade is stored as ws with a flag, and showing “WS” would name a transport other than the one configured on the server.

The last row is a server with a provider description: the description takes the place of the entire technical line. We give up to the provider only what it has something to replace with.

HomeScreen · _pickLocation · groupsGroups are entries in the same picker, not a separate screen. The user has one question: “what am I connecting through”. “Fastest” is just as much an answer to it as “Amsterdam”, and splitting them across two places means forcing the user to first choose a way of choosing. The section comes first because for most people that is the answer they need.

Each row's subtitle says what the group does, not what it's called in the config: “Lowest latency of 10 · rechecks every 5 min” instead of “url-test”. A type from someone else's YAML tells the user nothing, while “rechecks every 5 minutes” does, and it also explains why the server may change on its own.

We don't show the provider's `select` groups. They mean “let a human choose” — which is exactly what the list below already is; a second such row would be a second picker inside the picker. We expand them into their members and show only the types where the engine makes the choice: url-test, fallback, load-balance and relay (chain).

A group with no launchable server left after filtering is not shown at all: a choice that cannot work is worse than a missing one. Groups have no star — favorites are about servers, and the chevron is absent on purpose: tapping the row selects the group rather than opening a screen, and an arrow would promise the latter.

This is exactly what the live subscription sent, and that is more useful than a made-up example. There are three groups in the body; the picker shows two. RUSSIA is select, so by the rule above it is absent. The other two are url-test, and for both the provider wrote interval: 10: we won't poll someone else's URL from someone else's device every ten seconds, so the subtitle says “5 min”, not what was requested.

Membership is computed with filters, not from the list. PROXY has exclude-filter: 🇷🇺|🏳️ — of the fourteen listed members, ten remain; WHITE LISTS has include-all with filter: 🏳️, and those are the two. Ignoring filters is not an option, and not for tidiness' sake: exclude-filter here means “don't let this traffic out through a Russian server”, and a group assembled without it would send the person exactly where the provider was steering them away from.

The name is the provider's, and we don't improve it. “PROXY” says little, but renaming it means diverging from what the person sees for the same provider in other clients and in support. The subtitle explains what the group does — that's what it's for.

5 · Rule editor

A rule is one match type, one action and any number of values. A thousand exceptions are one rule with a thousand domains, not a thousand rows to scroll, drag and delete one by one. The rule opens as a full page instead of a dialog: a list pasted from a site needs room, and a page keeps the keyboard from covering the field. Text values are entered as a list — one per line, or separated by commas, spaces or semicolons — so the plain-text and comma exports of iplist.opencck.org and similar sites paste in as they are; nothing is fetched by the app. Countries and GeoSite categories are picked with checkboxes. For the engine, several domains or subnets become one RULE-SET with an inline list — mihomo looks those up in an index instead of testing a thousand lines in turn; a single value stays an ordinary rule line. Keywords, patterns, processes, countries and categories are written out one line per value: they are few, and mihomo has no indexed form for them.

8 · On demand (iOS / macOS)

Auto-connect handled by the system: rules are saved to the system settings, and the OS brings up the tunnel itself — when conditions match, after a network change, a reboot, and even if the app is not running. Rules are an ordered set (as in happ/Shadowrocket and like our rule sets): each has an action Connect / Disconnect / Ignore and conditions — network type, SSID, DNS search domain, DNS server address, URL probe. The first match applies. A manual “Disconnect” with on-demand enabled pauses auto-connect, the next “Connect” resumes it. Apple platforms only (NEOnDemandRule); on future Windows/Linux — a watchdog service + kill switch. Android has no such mechanism — instead there is the system Always-on VPN: a switch in the OS settings that the system itself triggers on boot and on connection drop; the last two boards are the Android variant of the section and an explanation screen.

9 · Error messages

Two formats, and the choice between them comes down to one question: will the user have time to act on this message. Toast — for what has already happened and needs no decision (subscription refresh failed, link copied): disappears on its own after 3 s, dismissed by tapping it, no close button — there's no point closing manually what goes away by itself. Error dialog — for what blocks work (failed to connect, account expired, no VPN permission): centered, with a scrim that intercepts taps until the message is closed with the 40 pt close button. It can't be skipped — unlike a bar at the bottom, which is easy to overlook — and a failure reason that vanishes after three seconds is useless. Neither format takes space in the layout: the screen must not jump because of a message. The wording always has two parts: what happened and what to do about it; the technical exception text stays in the logs, not in the UI.
